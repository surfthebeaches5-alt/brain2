Oh, So You're A
Sensitive Girlie,
Too?

The Gift and Challenge of Feeling Deeply

If you're someone who feels everything intensely (like the friend's slightly off tone in a text, the world's pain when you watch the news, the full spectrum of emotions in a single day…) you've probably been told you're "too sensitive" or "too emotional." I know I have.

We grow up seeing being sensitive or emotional as a weakness.

But the truth is, to feel deeply is to be fully alive. It means you don't skim the surface of life, you plunge into it. You notice subtle shifts, care intensely, and carry the weight of what others barely register.

That's a gift. But it's also heavy.

Because strong emotions can fuel connection, creativity, and clarity… or spiral into overwhelm and reactivity.

That's the paradox: what makes you emotionally rich can also leave you emotionally flooded.

What Emotional Regulation Is And Isn't

The goal of emotional regulation isn't to mute that richness. I don't want you to aim for feeling less. The goal is to create enough inner structure to hold that richness.

Think of it like a river: depth is beautiful, but it still needs banks to contain the flow. So instead of learning to feel less, we aim to feel fully while responding wisely.

This module is about learning how to move with your emotions, not drown in them. That's what the Stoics practiced: not coldness, but clarity. And that's what we're here to build.

What Actually
Happens During
Emotional Overwhelm

Your Brain on Big Feelings

When you're emotionally triggered, your brain goes through a predictable sequence:

  1. Trigger Detection (Amygdala):Something happens that your brain perceives as threatening.
  2. Alarm System (Limbic System):Stress hormones flood your system within milliseconds.
  3. Executive Shutdown (Prefrontal Cortex):Your rational mind goes offline temporarily.
  4. Fight / Flight / Freeze Response:Your body prepares for survival, not thoughtful response.

That's Not All…

For sensitive people, this process is often more intense:

  • Your alarm system is more finely tuned (you notice things others miss)
  • The emotional intensity can last longer
  • The recovery time may be extended
  • Multiple triggers can compound throughout the day

The good news is that understanding this process helps you realize that emotional overwhelm isn't a character flaw but a biological response.

And biological responses can be worked with skillfully.

The Space Between Trigger and Response

Remember when we talked about Viktor Frankl's revolutionary insight in module 1? Let me remind you of what he said real quick because it is crucial to understanding this module:

"Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and our freedom."

For most people, this space between trigger and response happens so quickly it feels nonexistent.

But with practice, you can learn to find and expand this space. This is where emotional regulation actually happens.

Here's what lives in "The Space":

  • The ability to pause and breathe
  • The chance to choose your response rather than react automatically
  • The opportunity to act from your values rather than your emotions
  • The power to break patterns that don't serve you
Building the gap muscle

Like physical strength, your ability to pause between trigger and response gets stronger with practice. Each time you successfully create space, you're literally rewiring your brain's response patterns. So, as with everything else, practice is key here. The Wise Mind:
Where Logic And
Emotion Meet

Meet DBT

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) was created specifically for people who feel emotions intensely.

While CBT focuses on changing thoughts to change feelings, DBT teaches you how to tolerate and work with intense emotions without being overwhelmed by them.

If you will, DBT can be seen as CBT's emotionally intelligent cousin: it combines the logical thinking skills of CBT with mindfulness and emotion regulation techniques that honor your emotional reality. Perfect for this module!

Beyond the False Choice

You've probably been told you need to choose between being emotional or rational, between following your heart or your head.

But there's a third option that integrates both (what a relief, right?).

The Three States of Mind (From DBT)

Emotion Mind:

  • Feelings drive behavior
  • Thoughts are colored by current emotions
  • Intensity is high, perspective is narrow
  • Example: "I feel rejected, so I must be rejectable"

Rational Mind:

  • Logic drives behavior
  • Emotions are minimized or ignored
  • Perspective is broad but may lack nuance
  • Example: "I shouldn't feel hurt by this, it's illogical"

Wise Mind (The Integration):

  • Both emotions and logic are honored
  • Feelings provide information, values guide action
  • Perspective balances both heart and head
  • Example: "I feel hurt, and I can choose how to respond thoughtfully"

Wise mind isn't a permanent state; it's something you drop into temporarily when you need to make important decisions or respond to challenging situations. It becomes more accessible with (say it with me:) practice.

Riding The Wave
Instead Of
Fighting It

Your toolkit

Just like you wouldn't go hiking without proper gear, you shouldn't navigate emotional intensity without the right tools. Over time, you will build your very own emotional regulation toolkit. I am here to introduce you to different tools and exercises, but only you can tell what works best for you.

Besides these worksheet-based exercises, emotional regulation tools also include breathing techniques, grounding exercises, supportive relationships, physical movement, or creative outlets, btw!

The Paradox of Emotional Resistance

The more you fight against difficult emotions, the stronger they often become.

What you resist persists.

Distress tolerance is about learning to surf emotional waves rather than trying to stop the whole ocean.

Key Distress Tolerance Principles

Emotions Are Temporary: All emotions, even the most intense ones, have a beginning, middle, and end. A feeling's initial chemical reaction lasts 90 seconds on average if you don't feed it with additional thoughts.

You Can Feel Uncomfortable and Still Be Okay: Discomfort isn't dangerous (even though our brain will tell us otherwise). You can be anxious and still function, sad and still show up, angry and still choose wise action.

The Goal Isn't to Feel Good, It's to Feel Skillfully: Instead of asking "How can I make this feeling go away?" ask "How can I be with this feeling in a way that aligns with who I want to be?"

Distress Tolerance vs. Emotional Bypassing: Pretending everything is fine is NOT distress tolerance. That would be emotional bypassing (which we don't want!). What we DO want is to build your capacity to awarely sit with the difficulty without being destroyed by it.

Practical Emotional Regulation for Daily Life

The STOP Technique (adapted from mindfulness practices):

  • Stop what you're doing
  • Take a breath
  • Observe what's happening inside you
  • Proceed with intention

The Emotional Weather Report: Instead of saying "I am angry," try "I'm experiencing anger." This small language shift creates space between you and the emotion, reminding you that feelings are temporary weather patterns, not permanent states.

The Wise Mind Check-In: When facing an emotional decision, ask:

  • "What is my emotion telling me?" (Emotion Mind)
  • "What do the facts suggest?" (Rational Mind)
  • "What would my wisest self do here?" (Wise Mind)

How Ancient
Stoics Handled
Intense Emotions

Stoics Weren't Emotionless Robots

Contrary to popular belief, the Stoics didn't suppress their emotions. They were actually very aware of their emotions and developed sophisticated practices for working with intense feelings skillfully.

Stoic emotional regulation techniques include:

  1. The Initial Impression vs. Judgment Distinction:Stoics recognized that you can't control your first emotional reaction (the "initial impression"), but you can control what you do with it (your "judgment"). Feel the feeling, then choose your response.
  2. Cognitive Distance Practices:The View from Above: imagine looking at your situation from a cosmic perspective. Temporal Distance: ask "Will this matter in 10 years?" Objective Description: describe the situation as a neutral observer would.
  3. Preferred Indifferents Applied to Emotions:Emotions themselves aren't good or bad; they're information. Your response to them is where virtue (or lack thereof) shows up.

You might have noticed already that these ancient practices align perfectly with modern emotion regulation techniques!

The Stoics were essentially developing cognitive behavioral strategies 2000 years before CBT was formalized… (which is crazy to think about, right?)

Working With
Specific Challenging
Emotions

Different Emotions, Different Approaches

While the general principles of emotional regulation apply to all feelings, some emotions have unique characteristics that benefit from specific strategies.

Understanding what each emotion is trying to tell you and how to work with its particular energy can transform your relationship with challenging feelings.

Here are some emergency reminders for navigating common emotions:

Anxiety

The Future-Focused Emotion:

  • Bring attention back to the present moment
  • Distinguish between practical concern and anxious rumination
  • Use grounding techniques to reconnect with your body
  • Remember: anxiety often shows up when you care deeply about something

Anger

The Boundary-Focused Emotion:

  • Recognize anger as information about your values or boundaries
  • Create space before responding (especially in relationships)
  • Channel the energy toward constructive action when possible
  • Distinguish between clean anger (information) and dirty anger (blame / resentment)

Sadness

The Loss-Focused Emotion:

  • Allow time and space for grief without rushing to "fix" it
  • Connect with supportive people rather than isolating
  • Remember that sadness often indicates the depth of your care
  • Practice self-compassion during vulnerable moments

Overwhelm

The Capacity-Focused Emotion:

  • Break things down into smaller, manageable pieces
  • Identify what actually needs immediate attention vs. what can wait
  • Use your support systems rather than trying to handle everything alone
  • Remember that overwhelm often signals growth or transition

Module 4
Exercises

After learning about all that theory, it's time to build practical skills for managing intense feelings with wisdom and grace. Each exercise helps you gradually expand your capacity to feel deeply while responding thoughtfully.

1. The Gap Pause Practice
This tool teaches you to find and expand the crucial space between emotional trigger and response. The moment when you can choose wisdom over reactivity is where your power lies.

2. Wise Mind Worksheet
This exercise helps you integrate both your emotional wisdom and logical thinking when facing decisions or challenges. Instead of being torn between heart and head, you'll learn to access the balanced perspective that honors both.

3. Distress Tolerance Menu
This exercise creates a personalized toolkit of healthy strategies for riding out emotional storms. Because sometimes the goal isn't to feel better immediately but to get through intense feelings without making them worse.

This tool builds your emotional awareness through gentle tracking and pattern recognition. Like a meteorologist studying weather systems, you'll become an expert on your own emotional climate (this is not as boring as it sounds, promise!).

These exercises work together to transform you from someone who gets swept away by emotions to someone who can dance with them skillfully. You'll still feel deeply (that's your gift), but you'll respond from wisdom rather than overwhelm.

2) THE GAP PRACTICE INSTRUCTIONS

Step 1: Recognition Training

(Practice this throughout your day)

  • When you notice emotional intensity rising, silently say: "I notice I'm having a strong reaction."
  • Rate the intensity: 1 (mild annoyance) to 10 (overwhelming emotion)

Step 2: The Physical Pause

(Choose one technique to practice)

4-7-8 Breathing: inhale for 4, hold for 7, exhale for 8. Repeat.
Box Breathing: inhale for 4, hold for 4, exhale for 4, hold for 4. Repeat.
5-4-3-2-1 Grounding: name 5 things you see, 4 you feel, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste.
Body Scan: quickly check from head to toe, notice where you hold tension.
Count to 10: slowly, giving yourself time to settle.

Step 3: The Choice Point

(Ask yourself one of these questions)

  • "What would my wisest self do here?"
  • "How do I want to feel about this interaction later?"
  • "What response aligns with my values?"
Be gentle with yourself

Don't be hard on yourself when in the beginning you're having a hard time finding the gap between trigger and response. That's totally normal! Building your "gap muscle" takes time. But the more you practice finding the gap, the easier it becomes.

You might want to start with these common trigger situations:

  • Someone interrupts you mid-sentence
  • You receive unexpected criticism
  • Plans change at the last minute
  • Traffic makes you late
  • Your text goes unanswered for hours
  • Someone disagrees with your opinion

Pick 1–2 situations where you typically react quickly and commit to practicing your pause technique this week.
